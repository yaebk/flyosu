What you are watching

The fly's view of the game is rendered onto its 8,452 photoreceptors. Notes become visible to it only in the lower half of the note area, 250 ms before they should be hit, which is its scroll speed. The dimmed upper half previews upcoming notes for you, not for the fly.

The network runs in 2 ms steps with its synaptic weights fixed. A small trained linear readout turns the activity of the descending neurons into four drive signals, one per key. A key is pressed when its drive rises through zero, held while it stays up, and released when it falls. Timing is judged with osu!mania-style windows (MAX, 300, 200, 100, 50, MISS), and hold notes are judged on both head and release.

The legs are an illustration. In the simulation the fly's output is those four key drives, read from descending neurons by the fitted readout. The real leg motor system is not modelled. Each drawn leg simply lands on its key for exactly the time that key was down in the replay.

The brain panel shows simulated activity, as change from each neuron's resting rate, for about 2,000 of the 19,367 connectome neurons, drawn at their real FlyWire positions. Each neuron is scaled to its own typical range, so the colours show when a neuron is unusually active, not how active it is compared with its neighbours.

This is a simulation of the fly's wiring diagram, not a living fly, and the readout was trained. It shows what a fixed connectome plus a small fitted readout can play. It says nothing on its own about whether the real wiring beats a randomly rewired one.
